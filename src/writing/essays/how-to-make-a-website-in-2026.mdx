How do you make a website, in 2026?

Well, what kind of website? And who’s making it? (And how much time do they
have? And how much money do they have? And will they need to maintain it a year
from now? Ten years from now?) There is no universal answer to “how to make a
website” because, as the archetypal senior engineer says, “it depends.”

I like to make websites, and I’m always curious how other people make websites,
so I tried making the same website six times using six different tools:

1. [Lovable](#lovable)
2. [v0](#v0) (Vercel)
3. [Replit](#replit)
4. [ChatGPT Sites](#chatgpt-sites)
5. [Claude Artifacts](#claude-artifacts)
6. [Val Town](#val-town)

This blog post is a sort of journal entry about my website-making experiment.

## Making a conference talk minisite

Real examples tend to be more useful than contrived demos, so for this
experiment I made a website for a conference talk I gave earlier this month
titled _Your corner of the internet_. My talk thesis was basically “I think you
should have a personal website.”

I wanted a simple reference minisite, but I didn’t want my example to be too
trivial, so I decided to add a comments section for attendees to post questions
and links to their sites. That’ll require a bit of interactivity, data
persistence, and maybe even authentication and moderation—more complexity than a
static site.

I fed the same prompt to each tool:

> I want to make a website for a conference talk I’m giving titled “Your corner
> of the internet” at Startup Cincy Week. It’ll be a workshop-style talk about
> creating personal websites, and I want to create a minisite at
> yourcorneroftheinternet.com (I already bought the domain on Cloudflare). I’m
> thinking a single page would be most legible rather than a slide deck-style
> multipage site. The page will mostly be used for links and resources that
> attendees might need during the workshop or want to reference after. The site
> should include comments so that attendees (and anyone else) can ask questions,
> share personal websites, or recommend resources. Do not write any of the
> actual material/content—I’ll fill that in myself. I just want you to create
> the bones of the site.

### Lovable

My first go is with [Lovable](https://lovable.dev), the ostensible leader in the
vibe coding tool space. It’s easy to sign up for a free account, and the
interface is functional and polished. I copy-paste my prompt and watch the
machine spin before it asks me a few questions. (If you’d like to see a live
unboxing, so to speak, I recorded an 11-minute
[knee-jerk video reaction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NzpjlP4nwJs) to
Lovable.)

I’ve intentionally omitted any design direction, so it presents a few visual
vibes to choose from. I opt for “handmade,” which of course is anything but. It
also asks how I’d like to handle authentication and moderation for comments.
Good of it to ask, as I’d been purposefully vague about that, too. After
answering its questions, the machine continues to churn.

I’m using up “credits” as the machine works—0.70 so far, now 2.30, now 3.10, oh
boy—and I have no clue what that means. There are no units. I’d prefer dollars.
Are credits just dollars? As it turns out, no. Lovable’s answer to the
frequently asked question “What is a credit?” describes credits abstractly as
units used to measure and pay for your usage. Moreover, the value of credits and
how quickly they’re used up depends on the subscription plan you’re on,
apparently.

And which language model is at work here? The fact that Lovable has chosen a
model for me, and hidden it from me, gives me a sense of their core audience:
not following the model Pareto frontier, doesn’t care about this week’s model.
(I later discover that I can select from dozens of language models. Apparently
they have their own Lovable AI Gateway with models from Anthropic, OpenAI,
Google, and even TypeSafe of recent
[Jev fame](https://x.com/CharlieMolthrop/status/2100330435351920806?s=20).) They
just want to make a website. To that end, 4 minutes and 55 seconds later, it has
produced a website!

Sign-in for the comments widget is broken, but otherwise it’s a plausible
website. I can easily publish to a `*.lovable.app` subdomain—have a look at
[yourcorneroftheinternet.lovable.app](https://yourcorneroftheinternet.lovable.app)—or
to my own domain if I upgrade to Pro.

I am totally sidetracked reacting to the unusual serif typeface it’s chosen.
Look at that funky ampersand; the _s_ looks upside down; and how about the
doohickey on that _f_ ascender. I’ll keep an eye on aesthetics and slop as I
compare the different tools.

My Lovable app is fullstack React, I learn, clicking around to find the code. It
seems a bit heavy for a single-page site what with dozens of files (I might have
created just three or four myself). The comments database seems to be using
Supabase, which required no intervention on my part to set up. There’s a whole
lot more—analytics, e-commerce and AI integrations, etc.—but for now I’ll move
onto my next trial.

Overall, I find the Lovable product well put together. It produced a website in
under 5 minutes—good enough, although I do have credit anxiety as the unitless
usage numbers tally.

### v0

[v0](https://v0.dev/) is Vercel’s well named vibe coding tool. I copy-paste my
prompt into the chatbox. After 11 seconds, v0 asks if I’d like to install
[Neon](https://neon.com/), because my comment wall needs a real database. Sure,
why not. (Does that mean I need a separate Neon account? Vercel creates one for
me, and later I’ll find a welcome email from Neon in my inbox.)

Unlike Lovable, v0 tells me which model it’s using: GPT-5.6 Luna, currently the
only one available on my free account. If I upgrade, I can access pretty much
any model, via the Vercel AI Gateway.

It makes fun little noises when it needs my attention, finishing a task or
asking a question. In another time and mood these unexpected bloops and dings
might bother me, but for the moment they delight.

v0 takes longer and pauses more than Lovable. It shows me some SQL to create a
database table for comments—LGTM. No website yet, but a button to “Continue
generation,” which I click. v0 is “Thinking,” and during the idle time I notice
a stink bug on the wall next to my desk. So I get up to deal with that, after
which I notice a plant that needs watering. So I go downstairs, fill up a
pitcher, water my plant, and return to my desk. v0 is stuck on the same step. Is
it working? There’s a shimmering text effect to signal some progress, but I’m
not sure, because there’s no count showing the seconds transpiring—or minutes
rather—and I also don’t see any credits or dollars expended yet. It finally
comes to a stopping point at 5 minutes 13 seconds. I’ve used 0.075 credits. But
again, what is a credit?

I do two more rounds of click “Continue generation” and wait until my website is
ready: 5 minutes 36 seconds for 0.070 credits, and 1 minute 29 seconds for 0.024
credits. At each of these pauses I have the opportunity to view the code diff,
and like Lovable it’s a big ole React app.

After 15 minutes, it’s done. It’s a website alright. There are some tells of the
AI slop design era we’re in, but it feels a little more homemade, like a
2010s-era site.

There’s a “Publish” button in the top right corner, just like on Lovable. I can
customize my `*.vercel.app` subdomain—`yourcorneroftheinternet.vercel.app` (now
expired)—or even to a custom domain on my free account. That’s a big point in
Vercel’s favor, as most products have a paywall in front of custom domains.
Vercel is a domain registrar too, so I can even buy a domain right in Vercel.

As with Lovable’s output, I’m distracted by font again. Letter spacing and line
height are extremely tight—neighboring letters and lines are overlapping. The
descender of a serif _f_ is creating an accidental ligature with the _i_ below
(on narrow screens). I’m not sure if a v0 system prompt or GPT-5.6 Luna is to
blame, but in any case I’ll want to select my own typeface.

The comments work. No authentication or moderation, probably inviting spam but
maybe fine for a one-day talk site.

As for v0 features, I poke around and find code diffs, a file tree, database,
logs, terminal, and more: integrations, environment variables, domains,
analytics. v0 is reminding me of something (it’s Lovable), until I find Design
Mode.

Design Mode is like Figma-meets-browser-devtools, with an AI chatbox on top. I

Lovable is a relatively new tool born in the vibe coding era, Vercel has been
around for about 10 years with a developer-first audience. Replit, the tool I’ll
try next, is another ~10-year-old developer tool, but I think with more of a
learn-to-code angle, so I’m curious if the Replit UX will fall somewhere in
between that of Lovable or Vercel.

### Replit

[Replit](https://replit.com/)’s onboarding is a bit more involved than Lovable’s copy-paste-go (I
already had a Vercel account). I answer a few questions, and before I even get
to the “What do you want to build today?” chatbox, they ask if I’ll upgrade to
Core for $20 a month or Pro for $100 a month. I stick with the Free plan and
paste my prompt.

Their free tier uses a combination of AI models chosen by Replit to build my
site—not sure which ones. Six minutes elapse, a bit slower than Lovable and much
faster than Vercel, and no stopping for questions or approvals. I don’t know how
many credits or dollars toward my free allowance. More cost anxiety. I guess
they don’t want to cause even more alarm with an ever-increasing dollar figure.

But alas, I don’t hit a paywall, and I have a website with some placeholder
content and a functional comments section (like v0, without moderation or
authentication). The website is pretty maximalist, sort of a combination of the
first two, design-wise. It has that same typeface that Lovable chose with the
funky _f_, and the letter spacing is super tight, like in v0.

Like in v0, I can toggle between a Design view of my website—a Figma-like
canvas—and a Build view with preview, file tree, and more tools: domains,
database, auth, integrations, secrets, analytics, etc. Replit’s output is also a
hunky React app with dozens and dozens of files. This is starting to grate on me
for such a simple site, although it may be a
[PEBKAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_error) prompting error. I would have
done totally the opposite by hand with maybe just an HTML file and CSS file and
SQL file and simple server.

Replit, like Lovable and v0 in its vibe coding cohort, works. I don’t really
_like_ any of my one-prompt websites yet, but it’s been a smooth and expedient
experience.

### ChatGPT Sites

[ChatGPT Sites](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/sites) is OpenAI’s quick live
website tool. I don’t get the sense OpenAI is heavily invested in Sites. It’s
still in beta, available on ChatGPT Plus and other paid plans (I’m accidentally
breaking my free rule here—whoops).

ChatGPT allows me to select from the GPT models: Astra, Luna, and Sol. I leave
the default (GPT-6.1 Sol) and paste my prompt. ChatGPT tells me upfront that it
“should take about 4 to 10 minutes to build and publish.” That’s quite helpful
compared to waiting in the dark with the other tools. And sure enough, ChatGPT
one-shots my website in 4 minutes and 17 seconds, the fastest yet.

As for design, again the H1 letter spacing is super tight. What’s up with that?
The style is certainly different than the other three. There’s definitely a
dollop of familiar AI slop, but it’s also—I don’t know, corporate? ChatGPT took
the same comment approach as v0 and Replit, sans moderation and authentication.
I can select elements on the site to request edits, but I’m actually not sure
whether it’s vanilla HTML, a React app, or what—the code is hidden, it seems.

I’m pleased with the simplicity and speed here, appropriate for my relatively
simple task. So far, this might be the experience I’m most likely to recommend
to a non-developer for a small website.

### Claude Artifacts

Alphabetically and logically [Claude Artifacts](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/17153992-what-are-artifacts-and-how-do-i-use-them) is the next tool to try after ChatGPT. Like
ChatGPT sites, Claude artifacts are limited on a free plan.

I drop in my prompt and Claude goes for it—no clarifying questions. It creates
just a few files—HTML, JS, SQL—as I would have done. And not only does it win on
architecture, it is my preferred design of the five so far.

Now, I don’t love how the website looks, but it isn’t overdone. Claude tells me

of CSS. Claude’s also chosen a single column layout that works on phones, “since
attendees will likely open it there.” Unlike the fullstack React apps, Claude’s
site is approachable to edit right away.

The simple HTML page was most in line with what I’d imagined, much lighter than
all of the other tools. I’m not sure precisely how much time elapsed–Claude
doesn’t show—but like ChatGPT, Claude was quite fast, quicker than the fullstack
vibe coding tools.

### Val Town

Last but hopefully not least, [Val Town](https://www.val.town/). I copy-paste the prompt into a chat with
Townie, Val Town’s coding agent.

Right away, I notice the same shortcoming: no time elapsed nor dollars spent as
Townie works. It’s only once it stops that I know 3 minutes have passed. I use
Claude Sonnet 5.5 for an apples-to-apples frontier model comparison. Townie
moved quickly, and the website works, but the design is nothing to write home
about.

The site’s complexity lies somewhere between the fullstack React apps and

For this particular site (and this particular developer), Val Town feels like
more than I needed when compared to ChatGPT and Claude, and on par with the
other vibe coding tools.

## It’s pretty easy to make a small website in 2026

I will say, it’s quite easy to make a simple website in 2026. I succeeded in
making a website in about 10 minutes with every tool I tried. I am not saying
it’s easy to make a _good_ website or a _big_ website, or that the copywriting
and design don’t still take time, but man: it is easy to ask a chatbot and
_poof_, here’s your website at whatever[dot]com.

Trying a tool for 10 minutes or an hour is not the same as sticking with it for
10 hours or mastering it over 100. I mostly did the 10 minute to one hour
version here, and even if I did this experiment over again, I’d get different
results. The slot machine nature of language models makes it all highly
variable. Not only may your mileage vary, but my own mileage may vary.

I am both optimistic and pessimistic about this moment for making websites. I’m
optimistic because end-user programming, the idea that everyone should be able
to customize their own software, seems more possible than ever. I’m pessimistic
because a slopsite, an AI-generated website with little to no human thinking
involved, is often worse than no website at all.
